CHAPTER 18 - THE COMPASS ROSE

That night, I couldn't sleep.

I lay in bed, staring at the ceiling, Marvin Marindale's letter playing on repeat in my mind.

*There is a threat coming.*

*Trust no one at Tanthrop Hammond.*

*Do not give up.*

The small black device sat on my desk, wrapped in a t-shirt so Mom wouldn't ask questions if she came in. I'd carried it home in my backpack, feeling like I was smuggling something dangerous.

Was it dangerous?

I didn't know.

All I knew was that Marvin Marindale—a man Grandpa said was brilliant and honest—had spent his last days hiding pieces of a machine around town and leaving clues for someone to find.

And somehow, that someone was me.

And Siggy.

And Raven.

I rolled over and checked my phone. 1:47 AM.

I had school in the morning.

And after school, we were going to the Atlas Library to find the next clue.

*Look for the star.*

I finally fell asleep sometime around 2:30, my dreams full of burned trees and compasses and voices whispering about wormholes.

(scene break)

The next day at school dragged by like someone had slowed down time.

In English, Ms. Kowalski talked about symbolism in *The Giver*, but I couldn't focus. My mind kept drifting to the letter, to the device, to Marvin Marindale.

In math, Mr. Hendricks gave us a worksheet on solving equations, and I finished it in ten minutes, then spent the rest of the class doodling triangles in my notebook and thinking about the Pythagorean Theorem.

At lunch, I found Siggy sitting at our usual table, scarfing down a burrito.

"Dude," he said through a mouthful of beans and cheese. "Did you solve it? Challenge 6?"

"Yeah. With Raven."

"And?!"

I glanced around to make sure no one was listening, then leaned in.

"We found something. A device. And a letter from Marvin Marindale."

Siggy's eyes went wide. "No way."

"I'm serious. He left us clues. There are more pieces hidden around town."

"Holy crap."

"We're going to the library after school. You in?"

"Are you kidding? Of course I'm in."

He took another massive bite of burrito, chewing thoughtfully.

"This is insane," he said finally. "Like, actually insane. Marvin Marindale was a real person. He died. And now we're following his treasure hunt?"

"It's not a treasure hunt," I said. "It's...I don't know what it is. But he said there's a threat. Something coming."

Siggy stared at me. "You really think it's real?"

"I don't know. But Grandpa said if Marvin left us a clue, we should take it seriously."

"Then we take it seriously."

(scene break)

After school, the three of us met at the Atlas Library.

Raven was already there when Siggy and I arrived, sitting on the steps with her backpack beside her. She looked up as we approached.

"Ready?" she asked.

"Ready," I said.

We walked inside.

The library was quiet, like always. A few people browsed the shelves, and Mrs. Kettering sat at the front desk, stamping books. She looked up and smiled when she saw us.

"Back again, Gavin?"

"Yeah. Just doing some research."

"Well, you know where everything is. Let me know if you need help."

We nodded and headed deeper into the library.

Once we were out of earshot, Raven whispered, "So where do we start? The letter said to look for the star."

"A star," Siggy said, looking around. "Like...a decoration? A symbol?"

"Maybe," I said. "Or maybe something in a book?"

We split up and started searching.

I walked through the aisles, scanning the shelves, the walls, the floor. There were old posters about reading, framed photos of the library's opening day, and a few displays about local history.

But no stars.

Siggy checked the kids' section, flipping through picture books and peering behind shelves.

Raven examined the front desk area, running her fingers along the edges of the old wood paneling.

After twenty minutes, we regrouped near the center of the library, frustrated.

"Nothing," Siggy said.

"Same," Raven said.

I looked around, trying to think like Marvin Marindale. If I were hiding a clue in a library, where would I put it?

Somewhere obvious enough that kids could find it.

But hidden enough that adults wouldn't notice.

And connected to knowledge—to books, to learning.

My eyes drifted to the floor.

The Atlas Library was built inside the old Brimley's Forge, and the original stone floor was still there in some places, covered with rugs and wooden planks. But near the center of the main room, there was a large circular section of exposed stone, polished smooth from years of foot traffic.

And in the center of that circle was an engraving.

A compass rose.

I'd walked over it a dozen times and never really looked at it. But now, as I stared down at it, I realized it wasn't just a compass rose.

It was a star.

An eight-pointed star, with N, S, E, W marked at the cardinal directions, and NE, NW, SE, SW at the diagonals.

"Guys," I said. "Look."

Siggy and Raven came over.

"The floor?" Siggy asked.

"The compass rose," I said, pointing. "It's a star."

Raven crouched down, running her fingers over the engraved lines.

"It's old," she said. "Really old. This was probably part of the original building."

"Marvin Marindale restored this place," I said. "He would've known about this."

"So what do we do?" Siggy asked. "Is there a clue on it?"

I knelt down beside Raven, examining the compass rose more closely.

The lines were deep and precise, carved into the stone. At each of the eight points was a small symbol—a letter or a number.

N. S. E. W.

And at the diagonal points:

7. 4. 9. 3.

"Numbers," Raven said.

"Four numbers," I said. "7, 4, 9, 3."

"Is that a code?" Siggy asked.

"Maybe. Or coordinates?"

"No," Raven said. "Coordinates have more digits. And they'd use degrees and decimals."

I pulled out my notebook and wrote down the numbers in order, starting from the top and going clockwise:

N - 7 - E - 4 - S - 9 - W - 3

"What if it's not a number?" I said. "What if it's a pattern?"

Siggy frowned. "Like what?"

"I don't know. A shelf number? A Dewey Decimal code?"

Raven's eyes lit up. "Wait. Dewey Decimal. That's how libraries organize books."

"Right," I said. "So maybe 7-4-9-3 is a section?"

"Let's check."

We stood up and hurried over to the shelves.

The Dewey Decimal System divides books into categories based on numbers. 000-099 is computer science. 100-199 is philosophy. 200-299 is religion. And so on.

700-799 is the arts.

We found the 700 section and started scanning the numbers.

740. Drawing and decorative arts.

I ran my finger along the spines until I reached 749.

"Here," I said.

There were only a few books in the 749 section—mostly old books about furniture design and interior decorating.

But one of them had a familiar label on the spine.

*Property of Marvin Marindale.*

I pulled it off the shelf.

The book was titled *The Art of Ornamentation: A History of Decorative Design*. It was old, with a cracked leather cover and yellowed pages.

I opened it to the front cover.

There, pasted inside, was the same ornate bookplate I'd seen before.

And tucked behind the bookplate was a folded slip of paper.

My hands shook as I unfolded it.

---

*Well done.*

*You are closer now. But the path ahead will be more difficult.*

*The second piece is hidden where children learn by doing. Look for the place where machines were once forged, now repurposed for young minds.*

*The code to unlock it is hidden in plain sight. Four digits. You will know them when you see them.*

*But be careful. There are those who seek what you seek. They do not have your best interests at heart.*

*Stay vigilant.*

*—M.M.*

---

I read it aloud to Siggy and Raven.

When I finished, Siggy said, "Where machines were once forged..."

"The children's museum," Raven said immediately. "It used to be part of Brimley's Forge too. They converted it into a hands-on museum for kids."

"The Marindale Children's Museum," I said. "Of course."

"And the four-digit code?" Siggy asked.

I looked back at the slip of paper, then at the compass rose engraved in the floor across the room.

"7-4-9-3," I said. "The numbers from the compass rose."

"So we go to the museum," Raven said. "And we use the code to find the second piece."

I nodded, folding the slip of paper and tucking it into my notebook.

But as I did, I noticed something that made my stomach drop.

Across the library, near the front desk, a man in a dark jacket was watching us.

He was tall, middle-aged, with sharp features and a cold expression. He stood near the local history section, pretending to browse, but his eyes were locked on us.

I nudged Siggy and tilted my head toward the man.

"See that guy?" I whispered.

Siggy glanced over. "Yeah. What about him?"

"He's watching us."

Raven looked, too, her expression tense. "Are you sure?"

"Positive."

We stood there for a moment, pretending to look at the shelves, but my heart was racing.

*There are those who seek what you seek.*

*Stay vigilant.*

"Let's get out of here," I said quietly.

We put the book back on the shelf and walked toward the exit, trying to act casual.

But as we passed the front desk, I glanced back.

The man in the dark jacket was gone.

(scene break)

Outside, we stood on the library steps, breathing hard.

"Did you see where he went?" Raven asked.

"No," I said. "He just...disappeared."

"Do you think he was following us?" Siggy asked.

"I don't know. Maybe."

"Or maybe he was just some random guy," Raven said. "We don't know for sure."

"Marvin's note said to be careful," I said. "He said there are people who want what we're looking for."

"People from Tanthrop Hammond," Raven said quietly.

We all looked at each other.

"So what do we do?" Siggy asked.

"We keep going," I said. "But we're careful. We don't tell anyone else about this. And we stick together."

Raven and Siggy nodded.

"Tomorrow," I said. "We go to the children's museum. And we find the second piece."

(end of chapter 18)
