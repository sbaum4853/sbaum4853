Backpack on my shoulders, I started biking  towards Grandpa’s house. I  had three hours before I was due at Raven’s—plenty of time to take the long way. The route took me past an old sports field, mostly abandoned now. The baseball diamond was being slowly erased by weed s  and,  strangely,  sunflowers.  That was something I’d noticed since moving to the south end of town: sunflowers everywhere. They grew in big, unruly patches, taking over empty lots and forgotten corners. Now that it was September, they stood tall and dry, their heavy heads sagging with seeds, petals faded and brittle in the sun. (illustration) I cut across the field, my tires crunching through dry grass, and found my thoughts drifting back to the note I’d found in my bedroom. A message is coming for you tonight.  The field opened into a small neighborhood of old houses and empty lots  (filled with more sunflowers) . I  always sped up here, pedaling hard until I hit Festival Lane, where I turned left toward Rainier Park.  Take it seriously. This is all for real.  That part was the creepiest. Because if the note really was connected to Siggy’s Raspberry Pi   and its message about alien invasion ,  then  not  taking it seriously would be  my immediate reaction, and here  was someone  telling me,  No , this is real , like he knew  exactly  how I ’d react. I kept riding. Rainier Park loomed ahead, dragging a whole pile of memories with it.  For weeks now , I’d been taking Corea Drive to Grandpa’s   just to avoid  Rainier   Park . And the tree. Fort Juniper. At least, that’s what Siggy said everyone called it now. The tree stood on the west side of the park, one of many tall pine s ringing  the open  field . A nd yes, it was a pine tree, not a juniper . W hoever named it clearly didn’t know  the difference .   Unlike the  other trees, Fort Juniper h ad a massive gap torn through its branches on one side , the  edges blackened with old burn scars. Siggy  had told me once ,  with complete certainty ,   that it  had been struck by lightning. He didn’t know the real story. (sketch of Fort Juniper in the park) The burn scar created an opening  just wide enough to  step through . Once  inside ,  the branches closed around you. The needles and limbs formed a thick curtain, and the world outside disappeared. Standing near the trunk felt like being  inside some ancient “mother tree” in a movie about elves or fairy sprites. Or  Avatar  (lol).  No wonder kids called it a “fort.” For months, for reasons I don’t know if I’m ready to  tell you about yet , Fort Juniper had been a  source of  anxiety for me. I’d avoided it so carefully that I’d started avoiding the entire park, rerouting my bike rides without even thinking about it. But now I knew that Raven lived across the street from Rainier Park. And months had passed since the incident that made me want to stay away.  What was done was done. I'd gotten away with it, guilty as I felt. No one knew the true origin story of Fort Juniper except me...and two kids who lived clear on the other side of town and had no reason to come here. I jumped the curb and coasted into the park,  letting  the steep downhill  carry  me toward the center. I rode straight to Fort Juniper and stopped at the edge of the branches. I  set my bike down in  the grass and stepped through the burn scar. (sketch of the inside of Fort Juniper) It was so much darker inside the tree, quieter too. You really did feel alone in there. I walked my bike up the drive, leaned it against the porch railing, and knocked on the front door.  “Good morning!” he said. “I’m surprised to see you here so early.” “I had some free time this morning,” I said. “Thought I’d come by.” “Well come on in. I just put the coffee on.”  I followed him inside.  Grandpa’s house was just as cluttered as his  workshop,  however it always felt cozy and comfortable in there. He had no TV, which gave the house an interesting vibe. Have you ever been in a house that doesn’t have a television? Totally different world. Feels like it’s disconnected from everyone else somehow, in a good way. Grandpa didn’t have a computer either, at least, not inside the home. He did have a cell phone and a laptop, but he  kept both in the garage at all times .  Those are tools meant for working so I keep them in the workplace,  he liked to say.  Without a television in the living, there was no central organizing principle of the place, no front to it, which made it oddly comfortable and cozy.  Grandpa’s  walls were lined with shelves  he’d made , and the shelves were lined with projects: a half-assembled clock mechanism, a wooden box with brass hinges he'd been restoring, rolls of old blueprints held together with rubber bands. An antique hand drill sat on the windowsill next to a coffee mug full of pencils. In the corner, a tall cuckoo clock ticked steadily, its pendulum swinging behind a little glass door. And the books! Grandpa must have had at least a  thousand books in his house.  But he only had bookshelf space for a few hundred, which meant that the rest spilled out into stacks that were everywhere. End tables, coffee tables, counters, the tops of the laundry machines, even stacks on the floor up  againt  the walls. He had lots of old books about machines and engineering, but also lots of history books. Grandpa loved history, and for the past year, he’d been reading biographies of every president in order of their presidency. He was currently on Harry Truman, which I knew because a giant book titled  Truman  sat alone on the end table by his favorite chair, a bookmark about halfway through it.  "Sit anywhere," Grandpa said, heading for the kitchen. "You want juice? Water?" "Water's fine." I dropped my backpack on the couch and looked around. Every time I came here, I noticed something new.
