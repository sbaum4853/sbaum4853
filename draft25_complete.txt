When I found  Dr. Kermit W. Sprocket’s Build Your Own Robot Kit  at a yard sale, I had no idea it would ultimately lead to me saving the world from an alien invasion. I just liked the idea of building a robot.  I also liked the price. Three bucks. It was one of those early September Saturdays, the kind that felt like the last gasp of summer.  The sun was  warm  but the air was cool, and the neighborhood smelled faintly of fresh-cut grass.  The  family  who lived  four houses  over  (I didn’t know their names—we were new in the neighborhood) , were having a yard sale .  I was there with my parents, rummaging through the relics of some other family’s past.  Baby clothes, coffee mugs, dusty exercise equipment, board games missing half their pieces, DVDs nobody had a player for anymore, old puzzles, old books, old puzzle books, a terrifying ceramic clown lamp, and… …a build your own robot kit? I found it tucked under a table, in a faded cardboard box. I  knelt down , slid the box out, and opened the lid. Inside, the parts were jumbled in a way that made it instantly obvious this kit had been torn through, used, at least a little, and repackaged using materials from the kitchen pantry. There were a couple of wheels, a plastic…something (the robot’s body, maybe?), a white plastic block that had a grid of tiny pinholes on its face, a bunch of  loose  electrical components , some more electrical components  in a sandwich bag, a coil of wires wrapped in a rubber band, a Lego, a  very strange pink plastic robot  figurine of some sort, and a booklet titled HOW TO BUILD YOUR FIRST ROBOT—printed in the same cheerful font that was on the box. I strongly suspected at least a few pieces were missing.  “Find something?” asked the woman running the sale. “Yeah,” I said, holding up the box. She smiled. “My son tried to build that years ago. Didn’t get very far.  Tell you what. The price says three, but you can have it for two .” Sold. An hour later I was on my bike, pedaling  to my  Grandpa’s  house .  My Grandpa, a retired inventor and engineer, was the perfect person to ask for help with a build-your-own robot kit. And now that I lived on the south end of town (my family had just moved the previous summer), it was easy to ride my bike to Grandpa’s house whenever I wanted. I arrived at Grandpa’s and rang the doorbell,  which  sounded  like a  fire alarm  mixed  with an air-raid siren . A minute later,  Grandpa  appeared. He was wearing his usual uniform of a button-down work shirt and blue jeans. He was wiping his hands with a towel. He had a smudge of machine grease on his cheek. “What’ve you got there?” he said, eyeing the box under my arm. “I found this at a garage sale,” I said, grinning. “Dr. Kermit W. Sprocket’s Robot Kit.  Two  bucks.” “Two bucks, huh?” he said. “Well, come on in. Let’s have a look at this two-buck robot kit.” He led me across the driveway and into the garage, which, to me, was the happiest place on Earth. Grandpa’s garage was meant to hold two cars, but if you asked him about that he’d say, “What a waste of space! I can park my car in the driveway!”  Instead, he filled his garage with  old  tools, machines, workbenches, half-finished inventions, and about a million strange objects whose purpose only he  knew .  The garage always  smelled of  solder and  saw dust, and I remember that, on this day, there was also a hint of mineral oil, which was the smell of Grandpa doing maintenance work on one of his big machines.  Grandpa had machines to that cut, drilled, sawed, and smoothed. He made things out of metal usually, but sometimes also out of wood or plastic. When I was little, and  Grandpa ran a business out of his garage,  he made little electronic contraptions for all the miners in town, but now he was retired and mostly made weird gizmos that only he understood.  I mostly just putter about , is what he would say if you asked him what he did all day in the  garage .  “What are you working on today, Grandpa?” I asked. “ Oh  nothing in particular,” he said. “Certainly nothing as exciting as a robot kit.” We went to his workbench. I set the box down and opened it. Grandpa leaned in and started  picking through the pieces.  He set aside the Lego and the pink figurine. “Yard sale detritus,” he muttered, which made me laugh.  To tell you the truth, I was already starting to like the pink figurine. He was a weird little guy. At  first  I thought he was a  robot  but he was  actually a  little different than that. He was like a person  made out of  bike lock  parts?  Wonderfully strange. Grandpa was still going through the rest of the kit.  “Hmm. No motor controller. No microcontroller. Without those,  your robot isn’t going to  walk or roll or do much of anything.” I felt my excitement deflating. Then  he held up the white plastic  block . “ Ahhh …but this is a useful tool. ” “What is it?” I  said . “It’s called a breadboard.” “A breadboard?”  “It’s for making circuits,” Grandpa said. “Back in the day, if an engineer wanted to prototype a circuit, he would screw components into a wooden bread-cutting board. Today we use these little plastic bricks instead, but we still call them breadboards.”  He kept going through the pile.  “ You have  a nice little assortment of  resistors  here, ”  he said. He held up a piece that looked like this.   “And jumper wires.” “And LED’s . ”   “Looks like you also have a connector for a 9-volt battery, which will be helpful, and of course you’ve got the instruction booklet. You can read that if you’d like. I won’t need it.” He tossed the instruction booklet aside. “What can we build with all that?” I asked.  “Lots of things,” said Grandpa. “Can we build a robot?”  Grandpa didn’t answer right  away, and  instead stared at the assortment of parts now spread out on his workbench.  “Tell you what, ” Grandpa said. “L et’s make a deal.” Classic Grandpa. His “deals” usually meant trading something I wanted—his wisdom, his tools, or occasionally cash—for something he wanted me to do, like chores, homework,  or,  one time, feeding his neighbor’s  pet  snake for a week. “Before you can make a robot, you have to understand circuits.” He tapped the white plastic block , the one he’d called a  breadboard . “This is the perfect place to start.” “So what deal are we making?” I asked. “You let me teach you about circuits , motors, and electronics , you do the hard work required to learn something new, and I’ll buy the parts you’re missing from this robot kit.”  This sounded like a pretty good deal to me.  “ So  I’m actually  gonna  be able to build a robot?” I  said . “In time,” said Grandpa. “But only if you promise to be patient. I’m going to show you the circuit Grandpa taught me to build that day.  You can just read along if you like, but it will be a lot more fun if you  actually  build  it. The materials  are easy to find . There’s even a kit you can buy  with  everything you need to do all the projects  in this book . Now, just so you know—this didn’t go perfectly  for me  the first time.  At one point I had the LED in backwards. Several times I had wires in the wrong rows, even though it looked like they were in the right ones.  B ut in the end, I got it to work, and you can too . Here’s  what you’ll need : - A breadboard (a plastic tool for prototyping circuits) - Some LEDs (get a few extras in case you burn some out) - Some jumper wires - A 330-Ohm resistor - A 9-volt battery and a 9-volt battery clip Step 1:   Clip the battery into your battery  clip . Now you’ve got two wires: one red, one black.   And here’s the first thing you should learn about doing electronics like this: Red  means positive , and  black means negative . SAFETY NOTE: You are now a real electronics engineer using real electronics prototyping equipment. All the electronics toys and gizmos you’ve ever used in your life have built-in safety features that you probably didn’t even know about. But when you are doing real engineering, like you are, the person designing the safety features is YOU.  And now that you’ve plugged in the clip to your battery, it’s time for you to  be in charge of  the first safety feature. Do not hold the metal wires of your clip together. This creates what’s called an unloaded circuit, which means the electricity is just flowing in a constant circle without any work to do. Without any work to do, the electricity will start to turn into heat. If you let an unloaded circuit sit for  a long time   you can generate enough heat to create a potential safety hazard.   Step 2:   Take the red (positive) wire and insert it into the  positive rail  on your breadboard. Something for you to know: Your breadboard has a plastic backing on it, which is meant to be there (don’t peel it off!). If you did peel it off (which you shouldn’t), you would see metal columns and rows. Look how, on the left and right side of the board, single pieces of metal go straight up and down, but in the middle, the metal is in short rows.  If you peeled away the backing of your breadboard, you’d see metal strips arranged in rows and columns. On the edges the metal is aligned in long columns. In the middle the metal is in short rows. These metal strips are how electricity flows through the breadboard. When we put electrical components in the breadboard, they  make contact with  the metal rails under the holes, then electrical current flows through the components and the metal rails. If you want electricity from the battery wire to flow to another component, we need to connect that component to one of the holes in the same column.  So  we will.  Step 3:  Use a jumper wire to connect the  positive rail  to  the  main grid, like this (show image): Step 4 : Attach one leg of the resistor to a hole next to your jumper wire, and another leg into a hole in a different row. Current flows through your resistor, just like it flows through your jumper wire, but the resistor has another job as well. The resistor provides  resistance  in the circuit. Some of the electricity will be turned into heat when it flows through the resistor, which results in what we call a “voltage drop.” This is important because a 9-volt battery provides too much voltage for a simple LED.  And now it’s time for another  safety note : Are you wearing eye protection? If you’re not, I suggest that you do. Sometimes when making a circuit, you make a mistake, and a component can overheat. Usually when a component overheats the material inside it starts to melt or smoke. It’s no big deal if that happens (electrical engineers jokingly call it “magic smoke” because it’s such a common part of making circuits). If that happens to  you, just disconnect the battery from your circuit, let everything cool down, and then throw away the component that has burned out.  But sometimes (very rarely, to be honest) when a component overheats, it does more than melt and smoke. Sometimes, if you made a wiring mistake that resulted in a component getting  really hot   really fast , it  pops . That’s when you want eye protection, because there might be tiny bits of plastic  flying about.  Don’t worry ,  it’s  very  rare  that you’ll pop a component , and when it happens it’s usually just a tiny ‘pop’ and a little puff of the magic smoke.   Step 5:  Now insert your  LED . This component is a little different than the others. LED stands for “Light Emitting Diode,” and that “diode” part means it is directional. It only works one way.  LED stands for  Light Emitting Diode.   A  diode  only allows  electrical  current to flow in one direction. Remember, the   longer leg   of an LED is  the positive leg . It  must face the  positive terminal of your power  source  or it won’t work. Step 6:  Stretch the other leg of the LED across the middle of the breadboard.  Step 7:  You’re nearly done!  Use another jumper wire to connect the other leg of the LED to the  negative rail  on the breadboard. Step  8 : Now insert the black (negative) battery wire into the negative rail like this: If your circuit’s working, your LED should be glowing right now.  But here’s the deal: for many of you, the LED isn’t glowing right now, and if that’s the case, go back to Step 1 and double-check everything. Common mistakes are inserting the LED backwards or not having components lined up in the same row. If you’ve double-checked it and it still doesn’t work, read this footnote. Once you get it working, you should write some notes in your favorite notebook (and if you don’t have a favorite notebook, you should get one). I haven’t told you about my favorite notebook yet, the Dragon Notebook. Don’t worry, I’ll tell you all about it soon enough. The Dragon Notebook is a HUGE part of the story you’re reading. For now, I’m just  gonna  show you the notes I wrote in the Dragon Notebook on  the day  Grandpa helped me build  a circuit  that lit  an LED:  I know it’s just a simple circuit, and all it did was light up one LED, but I was so, so excited when the LED started to glow.  A tiny red dot, no brighter than a Christmas light, but it felt like it lit up my whole face. “This is awesome,” I whispered,  admiring  the glowing LED. “I agree,” said Grandpa. “ This is a good start.  But before I go and buy you more parts, I want you to show me that you completely understand what you’ve just built. ” “I feel like I understand what’s happening here pretty well,” I said. “Okay, tell me.” “Electricity is coming from the battery and it’s flowing through these wires, through this resistor, through the LED, and it’s lighting up the LED,” I said. “Not bad,” said Grandpa. “But here’s a question for you: What is electricity?” I paused for a second, thinking about how to answer. I mean…I know what electricity is. We all do. It’s the stuff that lights up our houses and makes our computers work. It comes out of power outlets in the wall, or batteries. It’s  super fast  and powerful and makes zig- zaggy  lines as it moves. ” “It’s like…power,” I said. “In science, power is just a measurement of energy per second,” Grandpa said. “Try again.” “It’s like lightning,” I said.  “Lightning is indeed a form of electricity,” said Grandpa. “But what is it? My name is Gavin Holloway, by the way. Nice to meet you. I live in Brimley Forge, New Mexico. Brimley Forge sits halfway between Santa Fe and Taos along the old San Gabriel Trail (which is now Highway 618).  “Our city started out as a dusty little supply post called Gristle Creek,” Grandpa said. We were bouncing down Ninth Street in his old pickup truck, headed toward the Atlas Library, whatever that was.  “Like Gristle Creek College!” I said, which was one of the schools where my dad taught. “Precisely,” said Grandpa. “This was just a tiny stop in the Old West until 1829, when a man named Elias Crump discovered silver in Gristle Creek.” “My school’s named after Elias Crump,” I said. “Lots of things in town are named after him,” said Grandpa. “ All of this, our whole city, started up when he found silver, because back then, if anyone found silver anywhere, word got out. Our little town’s population quickly doubled, then tripled, then quadrupled.” “Because people were looking for silver?” I asked. “That’s right,” Grandpa said. “We live in a silver rush town.” I had heard of a  gold rush  before, but never a silver rush. Kinda cool, I thought. I’ve always thought silver was a little cooler than gold, even though I know gold is worth a lot more money. “By the 1840s, Gristle Creek was hopping,” Grandpa said, “but people were learning there wasn’t  actually a  lot of silver here. What we did have, in spades, was iron ore.” This part of the story I’d heard before. “We learned about that in school last year,” I said. “Everyone was pulling iron ore out of the mountains to make steel.”  “Yeah? What else did you learn?” “I learned that a man named Jonathan Brimley built the biggest steel factory, and that’s why the town is named after him,” I said. “Right you are,” said Grandpa. “Jonathan Brimley built a steel plant right in what’s now downtown. It was called Brimley’s furnace at first, because it was all about melting iron to make steel. But in time, that became just a part of his business. The other part, the bigger part, was the forge.” “What the heck is a forge anyway?” I  said .  Grandpa made a satisfied little grunt, like he had been  waiting  for me to ask that. “A forge,” he said, “is where metal is born.” I laughed.  “What do you mean, born?” “You ever seen metal when it’s fresh out of the mountain? ” Grandpa said.   “ It’s useless. It’s rock. Ugly, stubborn rock with bits of iron inside it. If you tried to build anything with it, it’d crumble in your hands. To make steel   you  have to  melt the rock, get the iron out, heat it up till it’s glowing like lava, and  beat the living daylights out of it .” “Beat it?” I  said . Grandpa grinned. “With  giant hammers .” I sat up straighter. “No way.” “Oh yes way,” Grandpa said. “Picture this: a big stone room, hotter than a dragon’s stomach. Fires roaring. Sparks everywhere.
